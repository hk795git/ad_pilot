How a horror short filmed in Ithaca attracted Hollywood, Jordan Peele, and the internet’s attention

By Phil J. Larson· Article

ITHACA, N.Y. — In spring 2022, while all the other Ithaca College students were busy with finals, Dylan Clark wanted to make a horror film. “We built the short around what would be scariest in a classroom with just the projector and having one character,” Clark says. “I was [also] taking an intro to Islam class that semester, and we were talking about how it was forbidden to paint the prophet and God in Islam. I thought that was really interesting, and I’m sure whether it was conscious or not, mapped onto it.”

That was the initial creative impulse that looks like it will lead to Clark’s idea turning into a fully-fledged Hollywood feature. After a summer spent carefully editing from home, Clark published “Portrait of God” on his YouTube channel in late August 2022. Over three years since being released, “Portrait of God” remains a viral sensation with nine million views and nearly half a million likes to date. The seven-minute short’s simplicity, measured pacing and lack of traditional jump scares are exactly why it became a hit; a young woman practices a presentation about a portrait that some people view as an ominous man with big eyes, while others can’t see a thing. The story explores the mystery surrounding faith and God, and how people can look at an empty painting and create meaning to try to make sense of the world around them.

While “Portrait of God” hasn’t started production or set an official release date, Clark teases the considerations that he and his co-writer, Joe Russo (Inheritance), have made when adapting the short into a feature-length screenplay.

“It’s been an interesting process to think about the kind of traps you can fall into with adapting a short film because we didn’t make the short film as a proof-of-concept — the hope was that it could stand alone,” Clark says. “When it comes to a feature, people have more expectations for how much you’re packing into a story. It’s been an interesting needle to thread, and it certainly has changed over time. The direction it’s ultimately culminating to is something as true to the short as possible, and trying to maintain a lot of the mystery.” Sydney Brumfield, an Ithaca College graduate who starred in the film as a young woman preparing a presentation, explains how “exciting” it is to see the industry recognizing Clark’s talent. She says it still feels surreal thinking about the success of “Portrait of God” when reflecting on its origins. It received 100,000 views in its first five days online. “I still feel like we’re just those college kids in their living rooms staring at the YouTube page, losing our minds,” Brumfield says. “Now, it’s surpassed what any of us ever thought it was going to be.”

What happens next

Online, fans have dissected the film’s themes and theorized about deeper meanings, making “Portrait of God” just the latest horror short to capitalize on YouTube’s popularity as a platform for young filmmakers to get noticed. Surina Belk-Gupta, an Ithaca College graduate who worked as the short’s assistant director, assistant camera operator and SFX makeup artist, is excited about this trend.

“Every college has a guest lecturer who’s like, ‘You have the tools to make a movie. Just make it happen.’ I don’t think that was really true for a very long period of time — you could make a movie, but getting eyes on it was not just something that could happen,” Belk-Gupta says. “It’s really cool that people are walking the walk now and saying they want newer filmmakers and people who haven’t gone up through the [traditional] studio system. It means the studios are really listening to what younger audiences are interested in.” While many people have discovered “Portrait of God” without knowing it was made at Ithaca College, with TikTok reaction videos contributing to its online success, the short has certainly become influential for current film students as well. Lucas Cavanagh, an Ithaca College graduate who worked as the film’s gaffer — although he notes “no one really had any official roles” on set — explains that he still gets Instagram messages from Ithaca students asking how he did the lighting.
